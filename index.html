About

I am a software engineer, researcher, and Chevening Scholar pursuing an MSc in Computer Science at the University of Edinburgh. My MSc thesis, Early-Season Detection and Cross-District Generalisation of Yield-Defined Maize Stress in Smallholder Farms in Eastern Uganda Using Earth Observation and Machine Learning, investigates how satellite imagery and machine learning can detect crop stress early enough to protect smallholder farmers from preventable losses. My research proposal for this work was awarded the Best Research Proposal in the Informatics Project Proposal course at the School of Informatics, University of Edinburgh.

I founded AgriX, a platform that empowers farmers in remote areas of Uganda to access profitable markets, purchase quality inputs, and receive timely weather and farming insights. I co-founded Pivosoft, a startup digitising small and medium-sized businesses across Uganda, and established Pivosoft Technology Academy to mentor young people in AI for agriculture, finance, and health.

With over six years of experience building production software across East African fintech, mobile money, dairy, and agricultural technology, I have delivered systems for organisations including Educate!, Vastel Health, Brueilex Group, Kimote Factory, and others. I graduated with First Class Honours as the top student in my cohort from Makerere University, where I later taught object-oriented programming under Dr Joab Agaba. Prior to Edinburgh, I served as a computer science lecturer at the International Business, Science and Technology University. I currently serve as a Venture Café Ambassador in Edinburgh, contributing to the innovation and entrepreneurship community.

Research Interests

My research applies machine learning and satellite Earth observation to detect crop stress early in smallholder farming systems across Sub-Saharan Africa. My MSc thesis focuses on Eastern Uganda, where I am developing models that identify yield-defined maize stress at the field level using freely available satellite imagery, and testing whether those models generalise across districts without expensive retraining. The goal is to turn satellite data into timely, actionable alerts that help farmers, governments, and development organisations respond before crop losses become irreversible.

More broadly, I am interested in how these methods can extend beyond crop monitoring to support food security assessment, climate risk mapping, and evidence-based development planning at local scales. Coarse national and district-level statistics often mask the realities of individual communities. My longer-term research direction is to help disaggregate these averages into village-level insights that reflect what is actually happening on the ground, enabling more targeted and effective interventions.
